faqdata:
  - question: "What is the difference between Gen 1, Gen 2, and Gen 3 wheel hub bearings?"
    answer: "Gen 1 wheel bearings are double row bearings without integrated flanges, designed to be pressed into a separate hub and knuckle. Gen 2 units include an integrated hub flange on one side, reducing assembly complexity. Gen 3 are fully integrated units with flanges on both the inner and outer sides, often including ABS sensor rings, offering the easiest installation and most compact design. Gen 3 is the most common type in modern vehicles."
  - question: "Are wheel hub bearings with ABS sensors compatible with all vehicles?"
    answer: "No, ABS-equipped hub bearings must match the specific vehicle's ABS system configuration. Some vehicles use active ABS with magnetic encoder rings built into the bearing seal, while others use passive tone wheels. The sensor type, number of pulses per revolution, and electrical connector must all match the vehicle's specifications. Always verify compatibility by vehicle make, model, year, and VIN before specifying a replacement hub bearing."
  - question: "What is the expected service life of wheel hub bearings?"
    answer: "Under normal operating conditions, quality wheel hub bearings typically last 80,000 to 150,000 kilometers (50,000 to 100,000 miles). Service life depends on driving conditions, vehicle type, load, and maintenance. Factors that shorten life include excessive load (overloading, towing), harsh environments (water, salt, dirt), improper installation, and aggressive driving. Premium quality bearings with proper sealing and lubrication, like the LD-408 series, are engineered to achieve maximum service life."
  - question: "What torque specification should be used when installing wheel hub bearings?"
    answer: "Torque specifications vary significantly by vehicle make, model, and bearing size. Always refer to the vehicle manufacturer's service manual for the exact specification. As a general guideline, axle nut torque for front wheel drive vehicles typically ranges from 150 to 300 Nm, while rear wheel drive and 4WD hubs may require different values. Wheel bolt torque depends on bolt size and vehicle type, typically ranging from 90 to 150 Nm. Proper torque is critical for bearing life and vehicle safety."
